Help & Deep-Dive Tutorial

This page explains each of the 15 sections of the ARS.md compiler — what the section is for, why the AI needs it, and examples of good and bad input.

1. Assessment Overview

Purpose:

The Assessment Overview establishes the identity and context of the assessment. It provides the foundational information that helps the AI understand what is being marked, who is responsible for the assessment, what qualification the assessment belongs to, how many marks are available, and whether the assessment is primarily practical, theory-based, or a mixture of both.

Why the AI needs this information:

Before the AI can evaluate student work, it must first understand the environment in which that work exists. A practical Web Development assignment is assessed very differently from a Psychology essay, Programming Logic test, or Business Management project.

The Assessment Overview provides the contextual frame that influences all later decisions such as evidence interpretation, feedback style, marking philosophy, and application of the rubric.

How this section influences marking:

The Assessment Mode field is especially important. If a lecturer identifies an assessment as "Practical", the AI expects evidence such as source code, screenshots, repositories, diagrams, applications, websites, databases, or project files.

If the assessment is identified as "Theory", the AI expects essays, written responses, arguments, research discussions, references and academic writing conventions.

Incorrect information in this section can cause the AI to approach the entire assessment from the wrong perspective.

Good Example

Module Name: Web Development
Module Code: WEDE5020
Qualification: Higher Certificate in Mobile Application and Web Development
Assessment Name: Portfolio of Evidence Part 2
Assessment Type: Portfolio of Evidence
Assessment Mode: Practical
Total Marks: 100
Marks within AI Scope: 70

Poor Example

Module Name:
Assessment:
Total Marks: 100

In the poor example the AI knows almost nothing about the assessment. The missing information increases ambiguity and forces the AI to make assumptions.

Common Mistake

Lecturers often assume information is obvious because they know the module well. The AI only sees the information supplied in the conversation. Missing contextual fields frequently lead to unnecessary clarification questions later.

Best Practice

Complete every field, even if it appears self-explanatory. Think of this section as the cover page for the entire marking exercise. If a new lecturer joined the module tomorrow, they should be able to understand the assessment from this section alone.

2. Purpose of AI Review

Purpose:

This section tells the AI what role it should perform within the marking process. Not every conversation requires the AI to act as a marker. In some cases it may only provide moderation feedback, while in others it may assist with student comments, quality assurance, or second-marking.

This section helps establish authority, responsibility, and limitations.

Why the AI needs this information:

A second marker behaves differently from a moderator. Likewise, a feedback assistant behaves differently from somebody asked to formally score work.

Without role clarity, the AI may begin performing tasks that were never intended.

Examples include scoring work that should only be moderated, offering feedback when only a marks review was requested, or evaluating assessment sections that are outside scope.

How this section influences marking:

The role selected here determines how aggressively the AI evaluates evidence and how much confidence should be placed in marks awarded.

  • Second Marker: Independently evaluates work.
  • Moderator: Reviews marking decisions and consistency.
  • Feedback Assistant: Focuses on student-facing comments.
  • Quality Assurance Reviewer: Examines assessment quality and alignment.

Good Example

Role:
Second Marker
Feedback Assistant

Role Description:
The AI should independently assess the Responsive Design
section worth 30 marks and provide detailed student-facing
feedback for identified weaknesses.

Poor Example

Role:
Marker

The poor example provides no information about what the AI should assess, how much authority it has, or what outputs should be generated.

Common Mistake

Selecting a role but failing to describe what that role means in the context of the assessment. Different lecturers often use terms such as "moderator" or "second marker" differently.

Best Practice

Assume the AI has never seen your assessment before. Write the role description in plain language and explain exactly what you want the AI to do, what it should review, and what it should ignore.

3. Marking Scope

Purpose:

The Marking Scope defines the boundaries of the AI's authority. It tells the AI exactly which sections of the assessment it is allowed to score and which sections remain the responsibility of the lecturer.

This is one of the most important sections in the ARS because it prevents scope drift during marking.

Why the AI needs this information:

Many assessments contain multiple components. For example, a Web Development Portfolio may contain HTML, CSS, JavaScript, SEO, GitHub, Documentation and Referencing components.

A lecturer may only wish the AI to review CSS and Responsive Design.

Without a clearly defined scope, the AI may inadvertently assess sections that the lecturer intended to review personally.

How this section influences marking:

Scope determines:

  • Which criteria receive marks.
  • Which evidence the AI analyses.
  • Which deductions may be applied.
  • Which comments should appear in feedback.
  • Which areas are ignored or reserved for lecturer review.

A well-defined scope improves consistency and reduces discrepancies between different marking sessions.

Good Example

Included Sections

Desktop CSS Styling (40 marks)
Responsive Design (30 marks)

Total AI Scope: 70 marks

Excluded Sections
            
HTML Structure
JavaScript Functionality
README Documentation
GitHub Repository
References

Poor Example

Assess the website.

This instruction appears simple but creates significant ambiguity. Does "the website" include responsiveness, functionality, GitHub activity, documentation, references, performance and SEO? Different reviewers may interpret this differently.

Common Mistake

Listing the sections to be assessed but forgetting to list what should not be assessed. Scope is defined by both inclusion and exclusion.

Best Practice

Think of the scope as a formal contract between the lecturer and the AI. Explicitly identify what the AI may score, what it may review without scoring, and what remains entirely outside its authority.

4. Full Rubric

Purpose:

The Full Rubric is the most important assessment document that the AI receives. It defines how marks are allocated, what evidence students are expected to provide, and what standards distinguish weak, satisfactory, strong and exceptional submissions.

While Section 3 defines what the AI is authorised to score, Section 4 defines how those marks should be awarded.

Why the AI needs this information:

The AI cannot reliably assess work without understanding the criteria against which the work is being judged.

Different assessments often use the same terminology but mean completely different things. For example, the word "functionality" in a Web Development assignment may refer to JavaScript behaviour, while "application" in a Psychology assignment may refer to applying theory to a case study.

The rubric provides the standards and expectations that allow marks to be allocated consistently.

How this section influences marking:

Every mark awarded should be traceable back to a rubric criterion.

If the rubric is incomplete, unclear, or unavailable, the AI is forced to make assumptions about what the assessment values most.

Strong rubrics reduce inconsistencies between markers, moderation challenges, and disputes regarding marks.

Good Example

Criterion: Gallery Lightbox
Marks Available: 5

Full Marks:
  Gallery is fully functional, responsive,
  accessible, and integrated throughout the website.

Partial Marks:
  Gallery functions but contains minor issues.

Low Marks:
  Gallery exists but functionality is incomplete.

Zero:
  No evidence of a gallery lightbox.

Poor Example

Gallery - 5 Marks

In this example the criterion exists, but there is no indication of what differentiates excellent work from poor work. This introduces subjective interpretation and inconsistency.

Common Mistake

Lecturers often upload only a marks table without the performance descriptors. The marks may be available, but the AI cannot determine what quality level is expected for each band.

Best Practice

Provide the complete rubric whenever possible, including mark bands and performance descriptions. If only the assessment document is available, allow the AI to reconstruct the rubric and confirm it with you before marking begins.

5. Submission Evidence Available

Purpose:

This section tells the AI what evidence is available for review. Understanding the available evidence is just as important as understanding the rubric.

Students often submit incomplete packages. A lecturer might have source code but no screenshots, screenshots but no repository access, or essays without the reference files used during moderation.

This section ensures that the AI understands what materials it can legitimately evaluate.

Why the AI needs this information:

The AI can only assess what it can verify.

If a student claims functionality exists but no evidence is supplied, the AI must know whether repository files, screenshots, lecturer notes, or other supporting materials are available before reaching a judgement.

Establishing evidence availability early reduces confusion and prevents accidental assumptions.

How this section influences marking:

The evidence supplied determines confidence in awarded marks.

For example:

  • A working GitHub repository provides stronger evidence than screenshots alone.
  • A complete essay provides stronger evidence than a summary document.
  • Source code is generally more reliable than screenshots of code.

If critical evidence is missing, marks may need to be reduced depending on the policy selected in Section 6.

Good Example

Evidence Supplied:  

  ✓ Source Code
  ✓ Screenshots
  ✓ GitHub Repository
  ✓ README Document
  ✓ Changelog
  ✓ Lecturer Observations
  ✓ Assessment Rubric

Poor Example

Files uploaded.

This provides no clarity regarding what files exist, which files are relevant, or whether enough evidence has been supplied to support the assessment process.

Common Mistake

Assuming the AI automatically understands what evidence has been provided. Different assessments require different evidence types, and lecturers frequently forget to specify missing or unavailable files.

Best Practice

Think like an external moderator. If somebody unfamiliar with the assignment received the submission package, would they immediately know what evidence exists and what is missing? If not, expand this section.

6. Insufficient Evidence Handling

Purpose:

This section defines how the AI should handle situations where evidence is missing, incomplete, inaccessible, corrupted, or impossible to verify.

Every assessment eventually encounters this situation. A student may forget to submit files, upload the wrong document, submit screenshots instead of source files, or include references to work that cannot be verified.

Rather than forcing the AI to make case-by-case decisions, this section establishes a consistent policy.

Why the AI needs this information:

Missing evidence is one of the most common causes of marking inconsistency.

Without guidance, one reviewer might award partial marks for unverified work while another reviewer awards zero. This creates moderation challenges and uncertainty.

A predefined policy ensures that similar situations are handled consistently throughout an entire cohort.

How this section influences marking:

The selected policy controls what happens when required evidence cannot be verified.

  • Award Zero: Missing evidence automatically receives no marks.
  • Award Zero and Flag: No marks awarded, but lecturer reviews the case.
  • Pause and Request Evidence: Assessment cannot proceed until clarification is provided.

This section also governs how partial evidence should be handled and how referencing concerns should be reported.

Good Example

Selected Policy:

Award Zero and Flag for Lecturer Review

Rationale:

If functionality cannot be verified through source files,
repository access or screenshots, marks cannot be awarded.
Cases should still be flagged for moderation review.

Poor Example

Use your judgement.

The purpose of this section is to remove ambiguity. Directing the AI to "use judgement" without providing a policy introduces inconsistency between students and marking sessions.

Common Mistake

Many lecturers know how they would personally handle missing evidence, but forget to communicate that approach to the AI. The result is that different conversations may handle identical situations differently.

Best Practice

Decide on your evidence policy before marking begins and apply it consistently across the entire cohort. If moderation is likely, "Award Zero and Flag" is often the safest approach because it preserves consistency while still allowing lecturer review.

7. Lecturer Observations

Purpose:

This section captures information known by the lecturer that may not be visible within the assessment submission itself. It acts as a bridge between formal submission evidence and the lecturer's first-hand observations of the student's work, behaviour, or submission process.

In many cases, the lecturer possesses important contextual information that cannot be discovered by reviewing the submitted files alone.

Why the AI needs this information:

The AI only sees the evidence supplied during the conversation. It does not attend classes, monitor submission timelines, view LMS activity, or observe interactions between students and lecturers.

This section allows important observations to become part of the review process without altering the evidence-based marking philosophy.

Lecturer observations should not automatically change marks unless they relate directly to the rubric, but they should inform concerns, recommendations, moderation notes, and future guidance.

How this section influences marking:

Lecturer observations are carried into the student's feedback under the Submission & Repository Concerns section.

They may also influence the lecturer's confidence in the final mark and help explain unusual circumstances that could otherwise be misunderstood.

Good Example

The repository was not submitted by the due date.

The student demonstrated a working system in class, but
the repository uploaded for assessment appears incomplete.

Several required files are missing from the final submission.

Poor Example

Student is lazy.

Student probably copied this.

Observations should be factual, professional and evidence-based. Subjective opinions, assumptions and emotionally charged language should not appear in this section.

Common Mistake

Including assumptions instead of observations. Statements such as "the student did not try" or "the student clearly used AI for everything" are interpretations rather than observations unless supported by evidence.

Best Practice

Write observations as if they may later be reviewed by a moderator, programme coordinator or external examiner. Focus on what can be observed, verified or documented rather than what is suspected.

8. Marking Philosophy

Purpose:

This section establishes the principles that guide marking decisions. While the rubric explains what should be assessed, the marking philosophy explains how judgements should be made when interpreting the evidence.

Think of this section as the assessment's "constitution". It provides the overarching principles that apply to every criterion and every student.

Why the AI needs this information:

Different lecturers often approach marking differently. Some favour a generous interpretation of evidence, while others apply stricter standards.

Without a clearly stated philosophy, identical student submissions may receive different results across different conversations.

This section helps ensure consistency by defining the standards that should govern all marking decisions.

How this section influences marking:

The marking philosophy influences how marks are awarded, how deductions are justified, and how uncertain situations are handled.

It affects decisions such as:

  • Whether partial evidence justifies partial marks.
  • How incomplete implementations should be handled.
  • How strongly the AI should rely on observable evidence.
  • How much confidence is required before awarding full marks.

Good Example

The AI must:

- Mark only observable evidence.
- Avoid assumptions.
- Apply the rubric consistently.
- Justify deductions clearly.
- Maintain consistent standards across the cohort.

The AI must not:

- Award marks for claimed functionality.
- Assume hidden work exists.
- Award marks purely for effort.

Poor Example

Be fair.

While fairness is important, the term means different things to different people. Effective marking philosophies provide specific guidance that can be applied consistently.

Common Mistake

Writing principles that are too vague. Instructions such as "be reasonable", "be flexible" or "be fair" may sound useful but provide little practical guidance when difficult marking decisions arise.

Best Practice

Focus on evidence, consistency, transparency and justification. If a lecturer, moderator and AI all follow the same philosophy, they should arrive at similar conclusions when reviewing the same submission.

9. Special Cases

Purpose:

This section defines exceptions, caps, limitations and recurring assessment-specific rules that are not fully covered by the rubric.

Every assessment eventually encounters situations where students have technically attempted a task but have done so in a way that prevents full marks from being justified.

Special Cases provide a mechanism to apply these decisions consistently across an entire cohort.

Why the AI needs this information:

Without predefined special-case rules, similar submissions may be treated differently in separate marking sessions.

This section converts commonly applied lecturer decisions into documented rules that can be applied consistently and transparently.

It is especially useful when marking large cohorts where similar mistakes occur repeatedly.

How this section influences marking:

Special Cases may:

  • Cap maximum marks.
  • Trigger automatic deductions.
  • Require lecturer review.
  • Define treatment of unusual submissions.
  • Standardise recurring moderation decisions.

These rules supplement the rubric; they should not replace it.

Good Example

Responsive Design

Maximum 15/30 if no tablet and mobile screenshots
are supplied.

Pseudo-Classes

Maximum 8/10 if only :hover is implemented.

Essay Assessment

Critical Analysis cannot exceed 50% if discussion
remains entirely descriptive.

Poor Example

Reduce marks if the work doesn't look good.

This instruction is subjective and impossible to apply consistently. Special Cases must be measurable, observable and defensible.

Common Mistake

Attempting to solve every possible assessment scenario through Special Cases. These rules should address recurring situations, not replace professional judgement or the rubric itself.

Best Practice

Add a Special Case only when you know the same issue is likely to occur repeatedly across multiple students. If you find yourself making the same moderation adjustment several times, it probably belongs in this section for future cohorts.

10. Output Format

Purpose:

This section defines how the AI should present its marking output. While many lecturers focus heavily on marks, the presentation of those marks is equally important. A well-structured review improves readability, consistency, moderation and student understanding.

The Output Format ensures that every student receives feedback in a predictable and organised structure.

Why the AI needs this information:

Different lecturers have different expectations regarding feedback. Some prefer marks only, while others want detailed criterion-by-criterion commentary and extensive student-facing feedback.

Without clear instructions, the AI may provide either too much or too little information for the intended audience.

Defining the format upfront creates consistency across the entire cohort and reduces the need for repeated formatting corrections.

How this section influences marking:

The Output Format determines:

  • How marks are presented.
  • Whether comments appear per criterion.
  • How subtotals are displayed.
  • The order of feedback sections.
  • The information provided to students and moderators.

It directly affects moderation readability and the usefulness of the generated feedback.

Good Example

Format B – Marks and Comments

Required Sections:

1. Student Identification
2. Marks Table
3. Total Mark
4. Feedback
5. Submission Concerns
6. Areas That Need Attention
7. Lecturer Moderation Notes

Poor Example

Provide feedback.

The poor example gives no structure, making it likely that outputs will vary across different conversations and assessment sessions.

Common Mistake

Focusing only on the marks table and forgetting the supporting narrative. Marks explain what was awarded. Feedback explains why the marks were awarded.

Best Practice

Decide how the final output will be used before selecting a format. Student-facing reviews generally require more detail, while moderation reviews may benefit from concise, high-signal outputs.

11. Lecturer Moderation Notes

Purpose:

Lecturer Moderation Notes are intended for the lecturer, moderator, or quality assurance reviewer rather than the student. This section provides a concise summary of the AI's confidence in the mark awarded and highlights any concerns that may warrant additional review.

Think of this section as executive-level assessment notes.

Why the AI needs this information:

Student-facing feedback and lecturer-facing moderation notes serve different purposes.

A student may need developmental guidance and encouragement, whereas a moderator may need to know whether repository evidence was incomplete, whether mark confidence is low, or whether unusual circumstances affected the review.

Separating these audiences improves the quality of communication.

How this section influences marking:

Moderation notes help:

  • Identify submissions requiring lecturer review.
  • Explain unusual marking decisions.
  • Highlight confidence levels.
  • Flag concerns about evidence quality.
  • Support future moderation discussions.

In large cohorts, these notes can significantly reduce the time required for moderation and quality assurance activities.

Good Example

Repository evidence is partially incomplete,
reducing confidence in responsive design marks.

Implementation appears consistent with screenshots,
but some functionality could not be verified.

Recommend lecturer review before finalising marks.

Poor Example

Good work.

This statement provides no useful moderation information and does not assist the lecturer in validating the assessment outcome.

Common Mistake

Repeating student feedback instead of providing moderation insights. The moderator already has access to the student comments. This section should focus on confidence, concerns and review recommendations.

Best Practice

Keep moderation notes short but meaningful. A moderator should be able to understand the key concern or confidence indicator within a few seconds of reading the note.

12. Assignment-Specific Overrides

Purpose:

Assignment-Specific Overrides allow lecturers to extend the standard ARS rules with instructions unique to a particular assessment. These rules are typically developed through experience, moderation discussions, or lessons learned from previous cohorts.

Overrides provide an opportunity to formalise recurring marking decisions that would otherwise need to be explained repeatedly.

Why the AI needs this information:

No template can perfectly accommodate every assessment. Certain modules, projects, essays or practical tasks often contain unique requirements that are difficult to express solely through the rubric.

Overrides allow the lecturer to customise assessment behaviour while still benefiting from the consistency of the broader framework.

They are particularly useful for practical assessments where recurring implementation issues regularly affect marking decisions.

How this section influences marking:

Overrides take precedence over general assumptions and help ensure recurring issues are handled consistently.

Examples include:

  • Maximum marks for incomplete implementation.
  • Requirements for multiple pages or files.
  • Caps applied when evidence is insufficient.
  • Additional moderation checks.
  • Assessment-specific interpretation rules.

Good Example

Override 1

Maximum 5/10 for External Stylesheet
if only one HTML page is supplied.

Override 2

Responsive Design cannot exceed 15/30
if no tablet or mobile screenshots are provided.

Override 3

Critical Analysis cannot exceed 10/20
if discussion is purely descriptive.

Poor Example

Use common sense.

Be stricter than normal.

These instructions are subjective and difficult to apply consistently. Overrides should be measurable, observable and directly linked to assessment requirements.

Common Mistake

Attempting to rewrite the rubric using overrides. Overrides should complement the rubric, not duplicate it. If an instruction already exists clearly within the rubric, it generally does not need to appear here.

Best Practice

Add overrides only when they solve a recurring marking problem. If you find yourself repeatedly explaining the same moderation decision across multiple students or cohorts, it likely belongs in this section.

13. Feedback Style and Sample Feedback

Purpose:

This section defines how feedback should be written. While the rubric, marks and moderation process determine what is being assessed, the Feedback Style section determines how the results are communicated to students.

Different lecturers have different expectations regarding feedback. Some prefer detailed developmental comments, while others want concise, high-impact observations. This section ensures that feedback remains consistent across an entire cohort.

Why the AI needs this information:

The same assessment outcome can be communicated in many different ways. Without guidance, one response may contain several paragraphs of analysis, while another may contain only a few bullet points.

Providing feedback expectations helps the AI align with the lecturer's preferred tone, level of detail, structure and language style.

Sample feedback is especially valuable because it demonstrates how the lecturer naturally communicates with students.

How this section influences marking:

This section governs:

  • The amount of feedback generated.
  • The tone used with students.
  • The balance between strengths and weaknesses.
  • The level of explanation provided for deductions.
  • The consistency of outputs across multiple students.

Well-defined feedback expectations improve student understanding and make moderation easier because similar issues receive similar explanations.

Good Example

Feedback Detail Level:
Extended Prose

Style Requirements:

- Professional
- Constructive
- Evidence-based
- Student-facing
- Explain all deductions
- Include improvement recommendations

Sample Feedback Supplied:
Yes (3 examples attached)

Poor Example

Give feedback.

While technically correct, the instruction provides no guidance regarding tone, detail level, preferred wording, student audience or lecturer expectations.

Common Mistake

Assuming that all lecturers define "good feedback" in the same way. Some prefer concise comments, while others expect detailed narrative explanations. The AI cannot infer these preferences reliably unless they are described here.

Best Practice

Provide two or three examples of real feedback that you would happily release to students. These examples often teach the AI more about your communication style than the written instructions alone.

14. Batch Processing Rules

Purpose:

This section governs how the AI should handle situations where multiple student submissions are reviewed within the same conversation.

Most marking exercises involve cohorts rather than individual students. As the number of submissions increases, consistency becomes increasingly important.

Batch Processing Rules help maintain fairness and ensure that standards remain stable throughout the review process.

Why the AI needs this information:

Without clear batch-processing rules, large conversations can become difficult to manage. Students may accidentally be compared against one another, marking standards may shift over time, or outputs may become inconsistent between responses.

These rules help the AI treat each submission independently while still maintaining alignment across the entire cohort.

How this section influences marking:

Batch Processing Rules affect:

  • How many students are processed per response.
  • Consistency across multiple marking sessions.
  • Treatment of individual submissions.
  • Moderation alignment within a cohort.
  • Output organisation and readability.

They are particularly valuable when marking practical assessments that may contain dozens or even hundreds of student submissions.

Good Example

Process six students per response.

Review each student independently.

Do not compare students.

Maintain consistent standards throughout
the cohort.

Follow moderator-approved examples where supplied.

Poor Example

Review all students.

This instruction provides no guidance regarding workload, output structure, consistency requirements or cohort management.

Common Mistake

Allowing submissions to be compared against each other. Assessments should be measured against the rubric and marking philosophy, not against other students within the cohort.

Best Practice

Define a practical number of submissions per response and keep that number consistent. This improves quality control, reduces cognitive load and helps maintain stable marking standards.

15. Assessment Files Supplied

Purpose:

This section acts as a final verification checklist before marking begins. It allows lecturers to confirm which assessment materials have been uploaded and which supporting documents are available for review.

Think of this section as the handover package presented to the AI before any assessment activity starts.

Why the AI needs this information:

Many marking problems originate from missing documents rather than poor assessment instructions. If important files are not supplied, the AI may be unable to interpret the rubric correctly, verify evidence, or provide meaningful feedback.

This checklist creates transparency by explicitly identifying what has been provided and what remains unavailable.

How this section influences marking:

The supplied materials directly influence:

  • Confidence in marking decisions.
  • The ability to verify evidence.
  • Feedback quality.
  • Moderation readiness.
  • Handling of missing information.

It also helps identify missing resources before significant time is spent reviewing student submissions.

Good Example

Files Supplied:

✓ Completed ARS.md
✓ CHANGELOG.md
✓ Assessment Brief
✓ Full Rubric
✓ Student Submissions
✓ Lecturer Observations
✓ Sample Feedback

Not Supplied:

✗ Memorandum

Poor Example

Everything required has been uploaded.

This statement may be true, but it does not help the AI determine what documents actually exist or whether certain files were unintentionally omitted.

Common Mistake

Assuming that the AI can automatically identify whether every required document has been supplied. The AI can only evaluate what has been shared in the conversation and may not know whether something important is missing unless the lecturer confirms it here.

Best Practice

Treat this section like a pre-flight checklist. Before marking begins, verify that all required files have been uploaded, are accessible, and correspond to the assessment being reviewed. A few moments spent here can prevent significant confusion later.

Final Thoughts Before You Start

The Assessment Review Specification (ARS) is more than a template. It is a communication framework between the lecturer and the AI.

The quality of the results you receive is directly influenced by the quality of the information you provide. A well-completed ARS gives the AI the context, boundaries, evidence expectations, marking philosophy and operational rules required to support consistent, transparent and defensible assessment decisions.

Before starting a new conversation, pause for a moment and consider:

  • Do I understand the purpose of this assessment?
  • Have I clearly defined the AI's role?
  • Have I clearly explained what may and may not be assessed?
  • Have I supplied the rubric and supporting evidence?
  • Have I documented any important lecturer observations?
  • Have I explained how missing evidence should be handled?
  • Have I provided examples of the feedback style I expect?

Most importantly, ask yourself:

Why am I having this conversation?

What outcome am I hoping to achieve?

What decisions do I need the AI to assist me with?

The ARS works best when it is completed deliberately and thoughtfully. Taking the time to provide good information at the beginning of the process will usually result in more accurate reviews, stronger feedback, improved moderation support and a more productive conversation overall.

Remember that the AI supports the assessment process, but responsibility for teaching, assessment decisions, moderation and final marks remains with the lecturer. The ARS simply helps ensure that both the lecturer and the AI are working from the same understanding.
